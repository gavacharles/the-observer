# Construction Dispute Risk Intelligence from Newspaper Corpora: An NLP Study of Uganda's Observer Newspaper (2016–2025)

---

## Abstract

**Background:** Formal construction dispute databases are largely absent in Sub-Saharan Africa, leaving procurement policy, contract design, and alternative dispute resolution (ADR) capacity without a locally grounded empirical basis. Expert survey methods that dominate global dispute research depend on practitioner networks that are thin, retrospective, and biased toward North American and European contexts. This article advances the conjecture that newspaper corpora can serve as viable risk intelligence proxies in data-scarce environments.

**Methods:** A full-corpus Natural Language Processing pipeline was applied to The Observer, Uganda's prominent independent investigative newspaper, covering 2016–2025. The pipeline crawled 32,117 sitemap URLs, parsed 5,530 articles, extracted 24,237 risk-bearing sentences from 3,242 relevant articles, and classified them into seven dispute-risk categories using a hybrid TF-IDF + LinearSVC and semantic-prototype fusion architecture. Extended analyses produced an actor co-occurrence network with community detection, sector-driver crosstabulation, Cramér's V association matrices, dispute co-occurrence within articles, temporal trend analysis with key event annotation, and a tripartite actor–driver–region linkage table.

**Results:** Newspaper dispute discourse is dominated by land and right-of-way conflict (62.6% of classified sentences), followed by delay and time overrun (14.5%), procurement irregularity (6.4%), quality and technical defects (6.1%), governance and oversight failure (4.3%), contract management failure (3.7%), and payment and financial disputes (2.4%). Publication-volume-normalised temporal modelling shows positive slopes across categories, but only contract-management discourse remains significant after Holm adjustment. Actor analysis identifies three distinct communities: a high-politics cluster (Government, Court, Parliament, President, Police), a technical-procurement cluster (UNRA, NWSC, PPDA, KCCA, Ministry of Works), and an energy-infrastructure cluster (UEGCL, Umeme, ERA, World Bank, Ministry of Energy). The top sector for dispute reporting is Roads/Transport. Spatial analysis confirms Central-region concentration (Kampala dominant) with persistent Northern, Eastern, and cross-border reporting. State Executive actors are most frequently co-mentioned with Land/RoW disputes, while Infrastructure Agencies concentrate in Roads and Delay categories.

**Conclusion:** The Observer corpus yields a rich, internally coherent, and analytically structured picture of construction dispute risk in Uganda. The study demonstrates that newspaper NLP pipelines can produce empirically grounded dispute taxonomies and actor-network intelligence as viable proxies for formal dispute records in data-scarce developing country environments.

**Keywords:** construction disputes, Uganda, newspaper NLP, media risk intelligence, dispute taxonomy, actor networks, spatial analysis, developing countries, infrastructure governance

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Problem with Current Dispute Risk Identification Practice

The dominant methods for identifying and classifying construction dispute risk rely on expert surveys, Delphi studies, and Relative Importance Index (RII) frameworks administered to practitioner panels. These approaches have produced influential global taxonomies of dispute causation, including recurring categories such as contract ambiguity, payment default, scope variation, design error, contractor insolvency, and project management failure. Yet these methods carry structural limitations that become acute outside the contexts in which they were developed.

First, the knowledge base is retrospective. Expert surveys ask practitioners to recall disputes that have already crystallised, been managed, and—often—been resolved. The resulting risk lists reflect past experience rather than current conditions. In fast-growing infrastructure environments where project types, delivery models, contractor landscapes, and financing structures are shifting quickly, this retrospective logic can reproduce outdated risk frames.

Second, expert surveys are circular. The taxonomies generated by early survey instruments are frequently used as the input vocabulary for subsequent instruments, creating self-referential factor lists that are validated by internal consistency rather than by independent evidence. Novel risk categories that have not yet been named in published instruments are systematically underweighted because respondents anchor to familiar item lists.

Third, the practitioner pool is geographically biased. Published systematic reviews of construction dispute research consistently identify North America, Australia, the United Kingdom, and parts of Asia as the dominant knowledge-producing regions. Sub-Saharan Africa is nearly absent from the global literature. This geographic gap means that the risk frameworks most frequently deployed in international infrastructure investment, aid-funded projects, and procurement guidelines are calibrated to institutional, contractual, and socio-political conditions that differ substantially from those in African developing country contexts.

In developing country settings, there is an additional constraint: formal dispute records are thin or inaccessible. Arbitration awards are rarely published. Court records are difficult to access systematically. Adjudication panels are uncommon. Claims databases do not exist at national scale. The consequence is not merely an academic knowledge gap; it is a governance consequence. Investment decisions, procurement policies, and ADR capacity investments are made without locally grounded evidence about the structure, frequency, or patterns of construction disputes.

### 1.2 Newspapers as an Alternative Risk Intelligence Source

This article advances the conjecture that newspaper corpora can serve as viable risk intelligence proxies in environments where formal dispute records are scarce. Journalism captures project distress signals in near real time. Reporters cover contractor defaults, government payment failures, land acquisition disputes, procurement controversy, parliamentary scrutiny of project costs, court proceedings against contractors or officials, and visible construction quality failures. These reports are created continuously, are publicly accessible, are digitised in growing volumes, and—unlike formal databases—do not require institutional cooperation to access.

The use of media data as a risk intelligence source has precedents in adjacent domains. Political risk analysis routinely uses newspaper event data to construct indices of political instability, conflict exposure, and governance quality. Financial risk research uses newspaper sentiment analysis to predict equity market volatility and credit conditions. Environmental monitoring research has used media reporting to identify industrial incidents and regulatory failure. Construction and infrastructure research has used computational text methods for contract clause analysis, court decision prediction, claims classification, and sentiment detection in project communication. However, no study has applied NLP to newspaper corpora specifically for construction dispute intelligence in Africa, or in any developing country context of comparable scale.

The gap is significant because the argument for newspapers as proxy dispute records has strongest force precisely where formal records are weakest. In environments with strong arbitration institutions, published awards, and accessible court records, newspapers supplement rather than substitute for formal data. In environments without those institutions—which describes much of Sub-Saharan Africa—newspapers may be the only readily available high-volume, longitudinal record of dispute emergence.

### 1.3 The Case of Uganda

Uganda presents a compelling case for this investigation. The country has pursued an ambitious infrastructure investment programme over the 2010s and 2020s, encompassing road construction under the Uganda National Roads Authority (UNRA), hydropower development (Isimba, Karuma), urban infrastructure in Kampala under the Kampala Capital City Authority (KCCA), water and sanitation expansion under the National Water and Sewerage Corporation (NWSC), oil infrastructure including the East African Crude Oil Pipeline (EACOP), and social infrastructure in health and education. This pipeline generates frequent procurement activity, contractor engagement, community interaction, and project-level conflict.

Uganda's formal dispute environment is constrained. The Public Procurement and Disposal of Public Assets Authority (PPDA) provides a complaints and review mechanism, but systematic data on procurement disputes are not publicly available at project level. Court litigation is slow. The Uganda Arbitration Centre exists, but construction arbitration data are not published. The Inspector General of Government (IGG) investigates procurement irregularities, but case-level findings are reported selectively. There is, in short, no comprehensive formal construction dispute record in Uganda.

The Observer fills part of this gap. Established in 2004, The Observer is one of Uganda's most prominent independent investigative newspapers. It has consistent infrastructure and public affairs coverage, an extensive digitised online archive, and a record of investigative reporting on procurement, contractor performance, land acquisition, and governance failures. Its editorial independence distinguishes it from government-aligned outlets, and its reporting depth on infrastructure matters differentiates it from general-interest publications. These characteristics make it the most appropriate single-outlet source for a systematic media-based dispute intelligence study in Uganda.

### 1.4 Research Questions

The study is organised around five research questions:

- **RQ1:** How do Ugandan newspapers report on construction disputes, and what dispute taxonomy emerges from this corpus?
- **RQ2:** What temporal patterns characterise construction dispute reporting in The Observer across the study period?
- **RQ3:** What spatial distribution of disputes is identifiable from newspaper reporting, and which projects and sectors dominate?
- **RQ4:** Who are the key actors in reported construction disputes, and what network relationships and community structures exist among them?
- **RQ5:** What correlations exist among dispute types, actors, sectors, and geographic locations as reported in the newspaper corpus?

### 1.5 Paper Contribution and Structure

This article makes three contributions. **Methodologically**, it delivers a replicable NLP pipeline for media-based construction dispute intelligence, including sitemap crawling, relevance filtering, hybrid sentence classification, actor and entity extraction, network analysis, spatial mapping, and sectoral association testing. The pipeline is designed for portability to other newspaper sources and other developing country contexts. **Substantively**, it produces the first empirical construction dispute taxonomy for Uganda, grounded in a decade of newspaper evidence rather than expert opinion. **Theoretically**, it advances the proposition that newspaper corpora can function as institutional risk intelligence proxies in data-scarce environments, extending media analytics into construction governance research.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature. Section 3 describes the methodology in full. Section 4 presents results across the five research questions. Section 5 discusses substantive and methodological implications. Section 6 concludes.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Construction Dispute Risk Identification: Current Methods and Limitations

Survey-based methods dominate construction dispute risk identification. The Relative Importance Index and its variants are the most widely used instruments, producing ranked lists of dispute causes weighted by practitioner perception of severity and frequency. Delphi studies are used where expert consensus is prioritised over sample representativeness. Archival methods—using court records, arbitration awards, or contractor claims databases—appear in a minority of studies and are concentrated in jurisdictions with institutionalised disclosure requirements, particularly the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia.

These methods produce the stylised facts that have become canonical in dispute research: that payment default, design errors, scope variations, differing site conditions, contractor insolvency, and poor contract management are the leading dispute causes internationally. These findings have been replicated across projects, sectors, and countries, suggesting that the core of the dispute taxonomy is relatively stable. However, the replication is not independent: subsequent instruments frequently adopt the same item vocabularies as predecessor studies, producing apparent consensus that may partly reflect measurement artefact rather than substantive convergence.

The retrospective limitation compounds the circularity problem. When practitioners are asked to identify dispute causes after the fact, attribution is shaped by the litigation or negotiation that has already occurred, by institutional incentives to frame causes in contractually defensible terms, and by memory reconstruction. The result is a risk knowledge base that is most informative about disputes that have been formally processed and least informative about disputes that are currently emerging, informally resolved, or systematically undercounted in formal records.

### 2.2 The Geographic Knowledge Gap and the African Context

Systematic reviews of construction dispute research consistently reveal a severe geographic concentration. Studies from Sub-Saharan Africa constitute a small fraction of the global publication inventory. Those that exist tend to focus on South Africa, Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya. Uganda, Tanzania, Ethiopia, and the broader Great Lakes region are almost entirely absent from the academic literature.

This absence has practical consequences. Construction projects in Uganda and comparable countries are often designed using FIDIC contracts, World Bank procurement guidelines, and risk matrices calibrated to international best practice. The dispute categories encoded in these instruments, and the risk weightings embedded in project appraisal and contract design, are derived from evidence bases that do not include the institutional, political, social, and contractual conditions that characterise infrastructure delivery in Uganda. Practitioners in Uganda therefore operate with borrowed risk frameworks whose local validity is assumed rather than established.

The constraints that create the data gap also shape the dispute landscape. Weak ADR infrastructure increases the frequency and cost of litigation. Limited enforcement capacity means that contractor defaults may persist without formal resolution. Political economy dynamics—including the role of political actors in procurement decisions, community mobilisation around land acquisition, and parliamentary scrutiny of project expenditure—create dispute pathways that are not well represented in global survey instruments designed around depoliticised, contractually formalised dispute processes.

### 2.3 Media Reporting as a Risk Intelligence Source

Journalism generates a distinctive form of dispute evidence. Reporters identify and investigate project distress signals through source networks, document leaks, parliamentary debate monitoring, court scheduling, community contacts, and site visits. The resulting articles represent a form of distributed intelligence gathering that is episodic, narratively framed, and editorially selective, but also continuous, publicly accessible, and longitudinal. These characteristics distinguish newspaper evidence from survey data (which is periodic and practitioner-bounded) and from formal records (which are institutionally constrained).

Media framing theory is relevant here. Newspapers do not report all disputes; they report disputes that are newsworthy. Newsworthiness is shaped by conflict salience, political relevance, financial magnitude, community impact, and editorial priorities. This means that newspaper dispute reporting systematically overrepresents high-salience, politically connected, and publicly visible disputes while underrepresenting informal, privately resolved, and low-profile disagreements. This selection bias must be incorporated into the interpretation of any newspaper-derived dataset. At the same time, the biases of newspaper reporting are not necessarily inferior for risk intelligence purposes compared to the biases of expert surveys, which oversample large contractors, formal projects, and resolved disputes.

Prior computational analysis of media in risk-adjacent domains provides methodological precedent. Political risk analysis has used newspaper event coding since at least the 1970s, culminating in systems like the Conflict and Mediation Event Observations (CAMEO) coding scheme and its successors. Financial risk research has used newspaper sentiment indices to predict credit spreads and equity volatility. Environmental monitoring has used media event data to track industrial incidents and regulatory failures. In each domain, the key analytical move is the transformation of unstructured journalistic text into structured event data through computational methods.

### 2.4 NLP Applications in Construction and Legal Domains

The application of NLP to construction and legal text has grown substantially in the past decade. Contract clause extraction and risk identification from construction contracts has been demonstrated using rule-based and machine learning approaches. Court decision prediction using text classification has been applied to building disputes, insurance claims, and adjudication outcomes. Named Entity Recognition (NER) for project names, parties, and monetary values has been developed in legal information retrieval. Sentiment analysis and framing detection have been applied to construction project communication, stakeholder reports, and social media. Topic modelling has been used to analyse construction safety incident reports and tender documents.

None of these applications has addressed newspaper corpora specifically for construction dispute intelligence. The NLP methods required—relevance classification, multi-category sentence-level classification, actor extraction, spatial mapping, and temporal aggregation—are individually well-developed. Their integration into a coherent pipeline for newspaper-based dispute intelligence is, to the authors' knowledge, novel, and has not been applied in African or other developing country construction contexts.

### 2.5 Theoretical Framework

This study draws on three theoretical perspectives. **Institutional theory** (particularly Scott's three pillars of regulative, normative, and cognitive institutions) provides a framework for interpreting newspaper dispute reporting as a socially constructed institutional signal. What newspapers report, how they frame it, and which actors they hold responsible reflects and reinforces the institutional norms governing infrastructure delivery in Uganda. Patterns of reporting therefore carry information not only about specific disputes but about the institutional conditions under which disputes emerge and are managed.

**Transaction Cost Economics** (TCE) provides the micro-level analytical lens. Construction projects are incomplete contracts executed under bounded rationality and opportunism. The dispute categories that appear repeatedly in the newspaper corpus can be read as evidence of transaction cost problems: land acquisition disputes reflect asset specificity and hold-up risk; delay disputes reflect measurement problems and incentive misalignment; procurement irregularity disputes reflect opportunism under weak institutional safeguards; payment disputes reflect enforcement costs under incomplete contracting.

**Actor-Network Theory** (ANT) informs the actor extraction and network analysis methodology. ANT treats the social and material world as constituted through relations among heterogeneous actors—human and institutional. The actor co-occurrence networks produced in this study are not merely descriptive; they represent the relational structures through which dispute narratives are assembled and stabilised in public discourse.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Research Design and Philosophical Positioning

The study adopts an exploratory mixed-methods design combining computational text analysis with qualitative interpretation. It is positioned within an interpretivist tradition insofar as newspaper text is treated as socially constructed dispute narrative rather than as a direct factual record of project events. The analytical frame is conjecture-testing: the core conjecture that newspaper corpora can serve as viable risk intelligence proxies is assessed against the evidence produced by the pipeline rather than assumed in advance.

The study is a single-case design focused on Uganda and The Observer. This choice is justified on theoretical grounds (Uganda provides a hard case where formal data are absent and newspaper-derived data may be particularly valuable) and practical grounds (The Observer was the only source with machine-accessible historical archives over the full study period). The design is transparent about this constraint and treats single-outlet findings as indicative rather than nationally representative.

### 3.2 Case Study Rationale: The Observer, Uganda

The Observer was selected on five criteria: editorial independence from government; consistent investigative focus on infrastructure, procurement, and governance; longevity of the digital archive (operating since 2004); accessible XML sitemap infrastructure enabling systematic historical crawling; and demonstrated depth of infrastructure reporting across roads, energy, urban infrastructure, and public procurement. The study period is 2016–2025, corresponding to the period of accessible sitemap records. No usable 2015 records were identified in the available sitemap history.

### 3.3 Data Collection

#### 3.3.1 Web Scraping Protocol

The Observer XML sitemap index was crawled using a Python pipeline implemented with `requests` and `BeautifulSoup`. The sitemap index exposed 33 post-sitemaps. Crawling and date-range filtering produced 32,117 URL-date pairs for 2016–2025. Article download used `requests` with retry logic, multi-selector HTML extraction for title, date, and article body, and a minimum word-count quality filter. A total of 5,530 articles were successfully parsed and retained. Metadata captured included headline, publication date, URL, and full article text. Deduplication was performed by URL.

#### 3.3.2 Keyword-Based Pre-Filtering

Before full article download, a 75-term URL-slug filter was applied to the 32,117 sitemap URLs. The filter lexicon covered infrastructure nouns, project lifecycle terms, dispute terminology, Ugandan institutional names, and sector terms. This retained 5,796 URLs for full parsing, representing 18.0% of the sitemap universe and providing high-recall throughput control. The goal was to exclude clearly irrelevant topics (sport, entertainment, international politics) while preserving the full scope of infrastructure-adjacent content for subsequent sentence-level filtering.

### 3.4 NLP Pipeline Design and Implementation

The pipeline comprises five sequential processing modules.

#### Module 1: Relevance Classification

A sentence is classified as infrastructure-risk discourse when it satisfies the relevance rule:

$$
I_{relevant}(s) = I_{infra}(s) \lor I_{risk}(s) \lor I_{proc}(s),
$$

where $I_{infra}$, $I_{risk}$, and $I_{proc}$ are indicator functions over curated infrastructure, risk-process, and construction-process vocabularies. The OR logic is theoretically principled: newspaper risk discourse often encodes project stress through legal, political, financial, or community language without repeating technical infrastructure vocabulary in the same sentence. Strict AND logic would systematically exclude politically expressed, socially embedded, or indirect risk signals. Applying this filter to 134,573 candidate sentences produced 24,237 relevant sentences from 3,242 relevant articles (sentence-level retention rate 18.0%; article-level retention rate 58.6%).

#### Module 2: Dispute Taxonomy Classification

The study employs a seven-category dispute taxonomy. Initial categories were drawn from international dispute causation literature and inductively adapted to the Ugandan context through iterative examination of high-frequency terms and themes in the candidate corpus. The resulting taxonomy is:

| Code | Label |
|------|-------|
| `land_row_dispute` | Land acquisition, right-of-way, compensation, eviction |
| `delay_time_overrun` | Project delay, time extension, late completion |
| `procurement_irregularity` | Procurement fraud, bid rigging, corrupt award |
| `quality_technical_defect` | Construction defects, substandard workmanship |
| `governance_oversight_failure` | Regulatory failure, audit findings, accountability breaches |
| `contract_management_failure` | Contract termination, abandonment, breach |
| `payment_financial_dispute` | Non-payment, budget shortfall, financial loss |

Classification was implemented using weak supervision from category keyword dictionaries to train a TF-IDF + `LinearSVC` classifier (the content branch), supplemented by a semantic-prototype branch using TF-IDF centroid similarity. Fusion resolved branch disagreements: `content_preferred` 61.34%; `agree` 29.31%; `semantic_override` 9.35%.

#### Module 3: Named Entity Recognition and Actor Extraction

A curated actor recognition layer used regular expression patterns matched against article titles and body text. Twenty-three actor entities were targeted, including UNRA, KCCA, Parliament, Government, President Museveni, PPDA, IGG, NEMA, NWSC, UEGCL, Umeme, ERA, World Bank, NSSF, EACOP, and line ministries. Actor co-occurrence within articles was used to construct the network.

Actor types were assigned to one of nine categories: State Executive, Legislature, Judiciary, Coercive State, Oversight Body, Infrastructure Agency, Utility/Infrastructure, International/Donor, and Private/Commercial. This classification enables sector-wide comparisons of how different institutional actor types relate to different dispute categories.

#### Module 4: Spatial Analysis

Location recognition used regex patterns for thirty named geographic entities. Each entity was assigned a regional classification: Central, Eastern, Northern, Western, National Corridor, or Cross-border. An article-level first-match heuristic assigned a primary region per article. Regional yearly heatmaps and trend series were generated to identify spatial concentration and temporal geographic shifts. Six infrastructure sectors were tagged: Roads/Transport, Energy, Water/Sanitation, Buildings/Housing, Oil/Gas, and Land/Urban. Sector tagging used regex patterns on article text and assigned the first-matching sector.

#### Module 5: Temporal Analysis

Sentence-level outputs were aggregated monthly and annually. Because newspaper publication volume changed materially across years, annual category counts were normalised by total yearly sitemap output. For each category $c$ and year $y$, the reported rate was:

$$
R(c,y) = 1000 \times \frac{N(c,y)}{A(y)},
$$

where $N(c,y)$ is the number of category-$c$ sentences in year $y$ and $A(y)$ is the total number of Observer articles in that year from the sitemap. Ordinary least squares regression was fitted to each normalised annual series ($n=10$ years). Because seven category-wise hypotheses were tested simultaneously, raw p-values were adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni sequentially rejective procedure. Residual autocorrelation was assessed with the Durbin-Watson statistic. Raw percentage growth from 2016 to 2025 was retained only as descriptive context and not used as inferential evidence. Temporal series were annotated with key events: the 2016 presidential election (February), the 2020 COVID-19 disruption, and the 2021 presidential election (January). Monthly series were analysed for peak detection using a prominence threshold at the 75th percentile.

### 3.5 Network Analysis

#### 3.5.1 Actor Co-occurrence Network

Nodes represent named actor entities; weighted edges represent co-occurrence within the same article. The full weighted graph was analysed for degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality. Community detection was performed using the Louvain modularity optimisation algorithm via the `community` package (with greedy modularity fallback using NetworkX where unavailable). This produced three communities corresponding to distinct institutional clusters in the dispute landscape.

#### 3.5.2 Dispute–Actor–Region Tripartite Linkage

A tripartite actor–driver–region table was constructed by matching actor hits, article-level dominant driver, and article-level region. This enables cross-tabulation and heatmap analysis of which actors are most associated with which dispute types and which geographic areas.

### 3.6 Correlation Analysis

Association between categorical variables—dispute driver, sector, region, and actor type—was assessed in two stages. First, an omnibus chi-square test of independence was conducted on each full cross-tabulation, with effect size reported as one table-level Cramér's $V$ computed from the chi-square statistic:

$$
V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n \cdot (k-1)}},
$$

where $n$ is the total count and $k = \min(r,c)$. Cramér's $V \in [0,1]$, with $V=0$ indicating independence. Following Cohen's guidance for nominal associations, values below approximately 0.10 were interpreted as negligible regardless of statistical significance.

Second, because Cramér's $V$ is a table-level measure and does not decompose to individual cells, cell-level departures from independence were identified using adjusted residuals from the chi-square model. Cells with $|z| > 1.96$ were treated as significant at $\alpha = 0.05$. This replaces the incorrect practice of reporting Cramér's $V$ for individual driver-sector pairs. Dispute category co-occurrence within articles was additionally visualised as a symmetric count matrix.

### 3.7 Validation Strategy

#### 3.7.1 Gold-Standard Annotation

Weak supervision generates training labels from keyword dictionaries. Evaluating a classifier trained on such labels against held-out pseudo-labels primarily measures fidelity to the dictionary rather than substantive category validity. To address this circularity, a manually annotated gold standard was instantiated.

A stratified random sample was drawn from the relevant-sentence pool, stratified by predicted category. The executed sample size is **$n=700$ sentences** (100 per category; minimum threshold satisfied for all seven categories). Annotation packs were generated as blinded coder templates (without model predictions), with an adjudication template and an unblinded key maintained separately.

Two independent coders are to annotate against the written coding protocol (Appendix B), blind to model outputs, followed by third-coder adjudication for disagreements. The reporting fields required for final insertion are:

- **Cohen's $\kappa$ (overall):** [PENDING manual coding completion]
- **Cohen's $\kappa$ by category (one-vs-rest):** [PENDING manual coding completion]
- **Number of adjudicated disagreements:** [PENDING manual coding completion]
- **Pseudo-label vs gold-standard agreement (accuracy and $\kappa$):** [PENDING manual coding completion]

All metrics above are produced by the gold-standard evaluation pipeline after coder files are completed.

#### 3.7.2 Pseudo-Label Benchmark (Diagnostic Only)

For model-family comparison only (not substantive validity), classification was benchmarked on 5,933 higher-confidence pseudo-labelled sentences (`confidence ≥ 0.55`). Model comparison covered five architectures: full TF-IDF + LinearSVC (production model), classical factor analysis, LSA 250-component + LinearSVC, LSA 200-component + Logistic Regression, and NMF 120-component + Logistic Regression. Cross-validation used five folds with stratified sampling. Precision, recall, and macro-F1 were reported as a diagnostic of separability under the weak-label regime.

### 3.8 Limitations

Four limitations are explicitly acknowledged. First, editorial selection bias means newspapers preferentially report high-salience, publicly visible, and politically connected disputes while underrepresenting informal or privately resolved disagreements. Second, the study relies on a single newspaper; generalisation to national media as a whole requires multi-outlet replication. Third, actor and location extraction uses curated regex patterns rather than fine-tuned NER, which may miss novel actor names, local project names, or non-standard spellings. Fourth, trend analysis covers ten annual data points, limiting the power of linear inference under high annual volatility.

The dominance of land and right-of-way dispute (62.6%) is the taxonomy's most striking feature. This category substantially exceeds its equivalent in globally derived taxonomies, where payment disputes and delay disputes typically rank at or near the top. This divergence reflects Uganda-specific conditions: the combination of rapid infrastructure expansion, large-scale right-of-way acquisition along road and pipeline corridors, and an urban property market in Kampala where compensation disputes are politically charged and legally contested. Land dispute in this context is not peripheral; it is the primary pathway through which infrastructure conflict becomes publicly visible.

Delay and time overrun (14.5%) ranks second. This is broadly consistent with global literature, confirming that delivery schedule failure is a persistent and visible dispute signal across institutional contexts. Procurement irregularity (6.4%), quality and technical defect (6.1%), and governance and oversight failure (4.3%) are clustered at similar levels, suggesting that the institutional and technical fault lines of Ugandan infrastructure delivery operate at comparable scales of public visibility.

Contract management failure (3.7%) and payment dispute (2.4%) rank lowest by sentence count, which likely reflects a combination of genuine underreporting (informal payment resolutions) and editorial preference for project-level political narratives over transactional financial details.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 The Ugandan Dispute Taxonomy in Global Context

The taxonomy produced by this study diverges from globally derived survey-based taxonomies in two important ways. First, land and right-of-way dispute—which rarely appears as a top-ranked category in North American, European, or Australian surveys—dominates the Ugandan corpus. This is not an artefact of the extraction method; it reflects genuine conditions of infrastructure delivery in Uganda where the combination of large corridor projects, community land rights, urban densification, and compensation disputes creates a distinctive dispute landscape. Second, payment and financial disputes—typically among the top three categories in global surveys—rank last in this corpus. This likely reflects a combination of underreporting (informal payment disputes resolved without litigation or public controversy) and editorial preference for land, governance, and procurement narratives that carry higher political salience.

The implication is that using globally derived dispute taxonomies as the basis for Ugandan procurement policy, contract design, or ADR strategy risks systematic misalignment. The risk mitigation measures that address the most visible dispute categories in Uganda (land acquisition protocols, compensation mechanisms, right-of-way clearance procedures) are different from those designed to address payment disputes or scope variations, which dominate global frameworks.

### 5.2 Media as Institutional Signal

Interpreting the actor network through Scott's institutional theory pillars illuminates the regulative, normative, and cognitive dimensions of infrastructure dispute governance in Uganda. The dominance of the high-politics community (Government, Court, Parliament, Police) in dispute narratives reflects the regulative pillar: disputes are narrated as processes requiring state authority, legal enforcement, and political resolution. The technical-procurement community reflects the normative pillar: disputes are also narrated as technical and procedural failures requiring institutional accountability through oversight bodies. The energy-infrastructure community reflects the cognitive pillar: disputes in the energy sector are framed through the lens of development financing and international infrastructure norms.

What is notably absent from the actor network is a strong private-sector cluster. Contractors, consultants, and subcontractors do not appear as dominant nodes. This suggests that Ugandan newspaper dispute reporting frames infrastructure problems primarily as state failures rather than as contractor failures—a framing that has policy implications for where reform attention is directed.

### 5.3 Methodological Contribution: NLP for Media-Based Dispute Intelligence

The pipeline demonstrated here is designed for portability. Its core components—sitemap crawling, sentence-level relevance filtering, weak-supervision category classification, actor regex extraction, and spatial tagging—do not require fine-tuned deep learning models and can be deployed without GPU resources or large annotated datasets. This makes the approach viable for research groups and government analysts working with limited computational resources.

The classifier comparison resolves a question of practical importance: which model family is most appropriate for this task? The evidence is clear that full high-dimensional SVM substantially outperforms all latent alternatives, and that LSA is a substantially better latent baseline than classical factor analysis. The practical recommendation is to use TF-IDF + LinearSVC as the production model and LSA + LinearSVC as the preferred reduced-dimension alternative if computational constraints apply.

### 5.4 Policy and Governance Implications

The findings support four specific policy directions. First, land acquisition protocols should be treated as the single highest-priority dispute prevention mechanism in Ugandan infrastructure delivery, given the overwhelming dominance of land and right-of-way dispute in the corpus. Second, the temporal evidence—showing episodic multi-category surges, but Holm-robust linear acceleration concentrated in contract-management discourse—suggests that dispute management capacity should be scaled to project-cycle risk windows rather than assumed to rise uniformly across all dispute types. Third, the actor-network finding that oversight bodies (IGG, PPDA) are most strongly associated with procurement and governance disputes supports the case for continued investment in these institutions as front-line dispute detection mechanisms. Fourth, the energy-infrastructure community's linkage to World Bank and international financing actors suggests that donor-funded project dispute management should incorporate community land engagement protocols as a standard requirement.

### 5.5 Escalation Assessment and Temporal Interpretation

The escalation pattern is best characterised as punctuated intensification rather than monotonic growth. Raw end-point percentage increases provide useful context, but inferential claims are based on the normalised rate series $R(c,y)$. Under this specification, only contract-management discourse shows a Holm-significant positive linear trend, while the remaining categories exhibit positive but statistically fragile slopes under multiple-testing control. Combined with the event-linked spikes and Durbin-Watson values near 2, the appropriate interpretation is not steady uniform growth, but a volatile dispute-reporting regime with selective structural acceleration.

### 5.6 Threats to Validity

**Internal validity:** Weak-supervision labelling using keyword dictionaries introduces the risk that the training signal reflects lexical stereotypes rather than substantive category membership. The high recall scores across all categories on the held-out test set mitigate this concern, but manual annotation of a gold-standard subset would strengthen the validation claim.

**Construct validity:** The study treats newspaper sentence counts as a proxy for dispute frequency and salience. The assumption is that dispute types with higher sentence counts are genuinely more prevalent or salient in the Ugandan infrastructure context. This assumption is plausible but not definitively established without triangulation against formal dispute records, which are unavailable.

**External validity:** Findings are specific to The Observer, Uganda, and the 2016–2025 period. Generalisation to other Ugandan newspapers, other East African contexts, or other time periods should be made cautiously and validated empirically.

## 6. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that newspaper corpora can be transformed into structured, analytically meaningful, and substantively informative construction dispute intelligence. Applied to The Observer over 2016–2025, the NLP pipeline produced a seven-category dispute taxonomy, temporal trend series, actor co-occurrence networks with community structure, spatial distribution analysis, sector associations, and correlation mappings. The core empirical findings are: that land and right-of-way dispute overwhelmingly dominates Ugandan infrastructure dispute discourse; that raw category visibility is higher in 2025 than in 2016 but publication-volume-normalised linear acceleration is Holm-robust only for contract-management discourse; that the actor network is state-centred and organised into three distinct institutional communities; that Roads/Transport and Energy are the highest-reporting sectors; and that Central Uganda and Kampala concentrate the largest share of spatial coverage.

These findings establish that newspaper discourse contains a coherent, recoverable structure of construction dispute information. The conjecture advanced in the introduction—that newspapers can serve as viable risk intelligence proxies in data-scarce environments—is supported by the evidence. The Observer corpus is neither a random sample of all construction disputes in Uganda nor a comprehensive formal record. It is a biased but structured and longitudinal signal that captures the high-salience end of the dispute spectrum with sufficient regularity to support taxonomy derivation, trend analysis, actor-network mapping, and sectoral association testing.

The most important limitation is single-outlet dependence. Replication with New Vision, Daily Monitor, and other accessible Ugandan media sources would strengthen the representativeness of the findings. Multi-outlet expansion is the highest-priority next step, alongside manual annotation of a gold-standard subset and geocoding for district-level spatial analysis.

The practical implication extends beyond Uganda. In any developing country context where formal construction dispute records are absent or inaccessible, newspaper corpora may be the only available longitudinal data source capable of supporting systematic dispute intelligence. The pipeline documented here provides a replicable, low-resource analytical architecture for deploying that intelligence in support of procurement governance, contract design, and ADR investment decisions.
