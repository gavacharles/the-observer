# Toward a Risk-Based Building Safety Regime in Uganda: A Comparative Legal Analysis of Uganda, South Africa, and the United Kingdom

## Abstract

Building safety regulation has shifted globally from narrow code compliance toward lifecycle risk governance, competency regulation, and stronger enforcement accountability. This article compares Uganda’s building safety legal framework with South Africa and the United Kingdom, focusing on primary legislation, amendments, and secondary legislation. The study uses doctrinal and functional comparative legal methods to evaluate the three systems across six dimensions: institutional architecture, technical standards and permitting, dutyholder competence, enforcement design, in-use building safety governance, and legal accountability mechanisms. Uganda’s framework is centered on the Building Control Act, 2013 and subsidiary instruments including the Building Control Regulations, 2020 and the National Building (Standards for Mechanical Installations in Buildings) Code, 2019. South Africa’s framework is centered on the National Building Regulations and Building Standards Act 103 of 1977, associated National Building Regulations and incorporated technical standards, with complementary construction safety controls under occupational safety regulation. The UK framework is centered on the Building Act 1984 (as amended), Building Regulations 2010 (as amended), and post-Grenfell reforms under the Building Safety Act 2022 and associated secondary instruments for higher-risk buildings. The comparison finds that Uganda has a coherent statutory base but remains relatively permit-centric and pre-occupation focused. South Africa has mature technical codification but uneven enforcement and fragmented interface governance. The UK has moved furthest toward lifecycle governance for higher-risk buildings, with explicit dutyholder architecture, regulator centralization, and resident-facing safety accountability. The article proposes a staged reform pathway for Uganda: clarify lifecycle dutyholder responsibilities, strengthen mandatory digital records, adopt risk-tiered oversight for high-risk stock, expand resident complaint channels, and improve enforcement proportionality and certainty. The contribution is a comparative legislative roadmap for adapting global building safety principles to a lower-resource legal and institutional context.

**Keywords:** building safety, comparative law, Uganda, South Africa, United Kingdom, secondary legislation, Building Safety Act 2022, construction regulation

## 1. Introduction

Building safety has become a central legal and policy concern following high-profile failures that exposed weaknesses in design oversight, construction quality assurance, fire-risk governance, and post-occupation accountability. In many jurisdictions, legacy building control models were designed primarily to ensure compliance at approval and completion stages rather than to manage evolving safety risks through a building’s operational life. This model has been increasingly challenged by contemporary evidence of systemic failures linked to fragmented accountability, weak competency controls, and insufficient enforcement integration.

For Uganda, the question is no longer whether a statutory framework exists, but whether the current architecture is sufficient for modern risk profiles, urban densification, mixed-use high-rise growth, and mechanical-system complexity. Uganda has enacted a substantial statutory framework under the Building Control Act, 2013 and relevant secondary instruments. However, comparative analysis is needed to identify which regulatory design features remain underdeveloped relative to jurisdictions that have moved toward integrated building safety governance.

This article compares Uganda’s legal framework (including subsidiary legislation) with South Africa and the UK. South Africa offers a long-standing codified building standards regime operating in an African institutional context with municipal implementation realities that partly mirror Uganda’s enforcement constraints. The UK offers a post-crisis reform model that has explicitly restructured regulatory accountability through the Building Safety Act 2022 and associated secondary legislation. Together, these comparators provide both contextual and aspirational reference points for Ugandan reform.

The research question is: **How does Uganda’s building safety legal framework, including secondary legislation, compare with South African and UK frameworks in terms of lifecycle safety governance, enforceability, and institutional accountability?**

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 outlines methodology. Section 3 maps each jurisdiction’s legal architecture. Section 4 presents comparative findings by regulatory function. Section 5 discusses reform implications for Uganda. Section 6 concludes.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Design

The study adopts a doctrinal comparative legal method integrated with functional and regulatory-governance analysis. At the doctrinal level, the article identifies and interprets primary statutes, delegated legislation, and related operative legal instruments that establish powers, duties, standards, and sanctions in building safety regulation. At the functional level, it compares how each jurisdiction performs equivalent regulatory tasks, including design approval governance, construction-stage compliance control, competency assurance, occupation-phase risk management, enforcement escalation, and resident-facing accountability pathways. This combined approach is necessary because formal legal equivalence does not always imply functional equivalence in practice, particularly where institutional capacity and administrative structure differ substantially across jurisdictions (Siems, 2018; Michaels, 2006).

The design is informed by critical comparative scholarship that cautions against rule transplantation detached from implementation context (Zweigert & Kötz, 1998; Berkowitz et al., 2003). Accordingly, the analysis does not treat legal sophistication as intrinsically superior. Instead, it evaluates whether legal architecture is likely to be administratively workable and risk-responsive under each jurisdiction’s governance conditions. This orientation allows Uganda-focused conclusions that are reform-oriented rather than merely descriptive.

The methodological framework also incorporates insights from regulatory theory, especially responsive regulation and risk-based regulation, which stress proportionate oversight, graduated intervention, and institutional adaptability (Ayres & Braithwaite, 1992; Black & Baldwin, 2010). These concepts are particularly relevant for building safety because enforcement systems frequently operate under finite technical and fiscal capacity while facing heterogeneous risk profiles across building categories.

### 2.2 Scope and sources

The scope covers legislation and operative legal architecture in force, or substantially in force, as of 18 May 2026. For Uganda, the analysis is centred on the Building Control Act, 2013 and identified subsidiary instruments under that statutory framework, notably the Building Control Regulations, 2020 and the National Building (Standards for Mechanical Installations in Buildings) Code, 2019. Where amendment concerns arise, the article relies on available consolidated legal versions and legislative metadata and treats point-in-time verification as a final submission requirement.

For South Africa, the analysis is centred on the National Building Regulations and Building Standards Act 103 of 1977, National Building Regulations issued under that Act, and associated standards architecture including SANS-based compliance pathways. Because construction safety governance intersects multiple legal regimes, the analysis also engages complementary occupational safety regulation relevant to construction-stage risk control and institutional interface questions.

For the United Kingdom, the analysis focuses on the Building Act 1984 and Building Regulations 2010 as amended, together with post-Grenfell reforms introduced under the Building Safety Act 2022 and associated delegated instruments governing higher-risk buildings, regulator roles, and dutyholder obligations. The UK discussion is England-focused for building safety institutional architecture, while recognising wider UK legal context.

In addition to legal texts, the article draws on regulatory governance, socio-legal, and construction safety literature to critically interpret how legal design choices are likely to shape implementation behaviour. This includes scholarship on compliance psychology, enforcement credibility, decentralised implementation variance, and post-incident institutional reform (Tyler, 2006; Parker & Nielsen, 2011; OECD, 2021; Hackitt, 2018).

### 2.3 Analytical dimensions

The comparison proceeds across six interrelated dimensions: institutional architecture, technical standards and permitting pathways, dutyholder model and competence controls, enforcement powers and sanction pathways, in-use and higher-risk building governance, and resident-facing information and complaint channels. These dimensions are treated as interacting rather than independent variables. For example, enforcement credibility is interpreted in light of dutyholder clarity and records architecture, while resident engagement pathways are analysed as both rights mechanisms and practical hazard-detection infrastructure.

The analytical logic is explicitly functional. A statute may be textually comprehensive but functionally weak if obligations are hard to operationalise, if institutional ownership is unclear, or if delegated instruments do not provide workable procedures. Conversely, relatively concise statutes may be functionally strong if delegated instruments and institutions provide clear accountability continuity. This functional perspective allows more realistic cross-jurisdictional inference.

### 2.4 Limitations

First, this is primarily a legal-structural and governance-design comparison rather than a statistical enforcement outcomes study. It therefore identifies probable governance effects rather than measuring incident rates. Second, municipal and devolved variation in South Africa and the UK can be substantial; the article focuses on principal national architecture and major institutional design features. Third, secondary legislation in building safety evolves rapidly, especially in post-2022 UK implementation, so all citations require final point-in-time verification before submission. Fourth, legal analysis cannot capture the full social dynamics of informal development and local political economy; however, these contextual factors are integrated into interpretation through relevant urban governance literature (Watson, 2009; Satterthwaite, 2020).

Despite these limitations, the method is appropriate for the article’s objective, which is to develop a rigorous comparative basis for Ugandan legislative and regulatory reform design under realistic institutional constraints.

## 3. Jurisdictional legal architecture

## 3.1 Uganda

Uganda’s building safety regime is anchored in the Building Control Act, 2013, which provides the statutory basis for building committees, approval control, occupation conditions, and enforcement powers. The Act establishes governance structures at national and local levels and seeks to formalize construction regulation in a context historically characterized by variable municipal enforcement.

The Building Control Regulations, 2020 operationalize procedural and compliance aspects of the Act, including applications, inspections, technical submissions, and administrative processes. The National Building (Standards for Mechanical Installations in Buildings) Code, 2019 introduces specialized requirements for mechanical systems, signaling regulatory recognition that modern safety risk extends beyond structural adequacy to system-level performance.

Uganda’s framework is therefore not legally empty; it contains a meaningful statutory and subsidiary base. However, its overall architecture remains predominantly approval-and-compliance oriented. The strongest legal focus is on pre-construction and construction-stage control, with less explicit systematization of in-use risk governance for complex occupied stock. In addition, while mechanical systems are addressed through specific code instruments, comprehensive risk-tiered governance for higher-risk residential stock appears less developed when compared to recent UK reforms.

The Ugandan framework also appears to rely heavily on administrative institutions whose performance may vary by local capacity. This creates potential implementation asymmetry: statutory powers exist, but practical enforcement intensity and consistency may diverge between jurisdictions.

From a governance-design perspective, Uganda’s framework can be read as a first-generation formalisation model. It establishes legal authority, procedural gateways, and institutional visibility, which are indispensable for any safety system. However, first-generation models often prioritise control of new development over governance of occupied stock. This sequencing is understandable in rapidly urbanising states, but it creates regulatory lag where densification and system complexity outpace occupation-phase legal design (World Bank, 2020; Meacham, 2010). In practical terms, hazards linked to maintenance failure, unauthorised alterations, or management deterioration may remain under-addressed unless legal obligations continue beyond completion.

The Ugandan secondary architecture, while valuable, also illustrates the distinction between technical and governance delegation. Technical delegation specifies requirements for compliance objects, such as mechanical systems, while governance delegation specifies who must do what, when, with what records, and under what reporting and review duties over time. Comparative evidence suggests that second-generation reform requires stronger governance delegation, especially for high-consequence categories where consequence of failure is large relative to regulatory capacity (Black & Baldwin, 2010; OECD, 2021).

Another critical issue is information continuity. In many construction systems, records generated during design and construction are not fully structured for occupation-phase safety governance. When incidents or complaints arise later, regulators may face fragmented evidentiary trails, weakening both enforcement and corrective intervention quality. Uganda’s regime could therefore benefit from explicit legal architecture for digital handover records, version-controlled compliance history, and mandatory update obligations for safety-critical modifications. These tools are increasingly treated as core elements of modern safety governance rather than optional administrative improvements (Gann et al., 2023; HSE, 2024).

Institutional legitimacy is also relevant. Compliance research indicates that legal obedience is influenced not only by sanction fear but by perceived fairness, predictability, and accessibility of regulatory processes (Tyler, 2006; Parker & Nielsen, 2011). If building control processes are perceived as opaque, inconsistent, or difficult to navigate, actors may revert to informal pathways. Reform in Uganda should therefore include process transparency measures and clear rights-based communication standards, particularly where public safety risk intersects with rapid urban growth and mixed formal-informal development dynamics.

## 3.2 South Africa

South Africa’s building safety system is built around the National Building Regulations and Building Standards Act 103 of 1977. The Act establishes the legal requirement that building work satisfy prescribed standards and creates municipal gatekeeping through plan approval and compliance control. Over time, the technical architecture has become strongly codified through the National Building Regulations and SANS-linked technical standards.

A notable strength of the South African model is technical depth and standardization. Incorporation of standardized technical rules supports consistency and technical predictability, at least in principle. This has value for designers, contractors, and regulators by reducing interpretive uncertainty in core compliance matters.

However, South Africa’s system also illustrates a common challenge in code-heavy jurisdictions: legal and technical sophistication does not automatically translate into consistent enforcement outcomes. Municipal capacity differences, resource constraints, and uneven inspection intensity can produce implementation gaps. Furthermore, construction-site safety and occupational risk are partly governed through separate occupational safety law and regulations, creating a multi-regime landscape that requires institutional coordination to avoid fragmented oversight.

Thus, South Africa offers an important comparative lesson for Uganda: codification is necessary but not sufficient. Enforcement infrastructure, municipal capability, and inter-agency coordination determine whether statutory design becomes practical safety performance.

South Africa also offers insight into the strengths and constraints of standards-integrated regulation. Standards incorporation can improve technical coherence and support professional practice, but it can also create accessibility challenges where standard interpretation requires high technical literacy or costly compliance pathways. In lower-capacity municipalities, this may produce uneven practical application, with well-resourced actors navigating standards effectively while smaller actors struggle. Comparative policy analysis therefore suggests that codification should be paired with implementation support tools, such as guidance notes, simplified pathways for lower-risk categories, and technical support mechanisms for local authorities (OECD, 2021; World Bank, 2020).

The relationship between municipal autonomy and national consistency is another instructive issue. South Africa’s legal architecture provides national standards, but local implementation remains decisive in outcomes. This has generated a recurrent policy tension between standardisation and contextual flexibility. Excessive central rigidity can be impractical in variable local conditions, while excessive local discretion can undermine legal certainty. Uganda is likely to face similar tensions. A constructive lesson is to define non-negotiable national safety minima while enabling calibrated local adaptation in procedural execution.

South Africa also demonstrates that occupational safety and building control interfaces are not merely technical overlaps but accountability challenges. Where incident causation spans design, construction method, and management practice, legal systems that separate these domains too sharply may struggle to assign responsibility coherently. Integrated inspection protocols, cross-referencing duties, and coordinated reporting channels can mitigate these interface failures. Uganda’s reform strategy should explicitly anticipate these cross-regime coordination requirements.

Finally, South African experience underscores the importance of political economy. Regulatory outcomes are shaped by procurement pressures, contractor market structure, and local governance incentives. Even technically sound rules may underperform where enforcement institutions are politically constrained or fiscally stretched. This strengthens the argument for risk-prioritised supervision in Uganda rather than universal high-intensity controls that are difficult to sustain.

## 3.3 United Kingdom

The UK’s legal architecture has undergone fundamental reconfiguration following the Grenfell Tower disaster and subsequent reviews. The Building Safety Act 2022 introduced a new governance paradigm that extends beyond traditional building control approval to include risk-based, lifecycle-oriented duties for higher-risk buildings. The Act established or consolidated a stronger regulator-centered model and introduced explicit dutyholder structures, mandatory information requirements, and stronger enforcement pathways.

The UK post-2022 framework is notable for three features. First, it differentiates higher-risk buildings as a distinct regulatory category requiring additional controls at design, construction, and occupation stages. Second, it formalizes accountability roles, including principal accountable person concepts for occupied higher-risk stock, and ties these roles to mandatory safety case and information duties. Third, it builds resident-facing governance mechanisms, including complaint and engagement functions, into legal architecture.

Secondary legislation under the UK framework operationalizes these statutory reforms through procedures, definitions, competence requirements, and building control transitions. Building Regulations 2010, as amended, remain central for technical compliance, but are now nested within a stronger risk-governance ecosystem for higher-risk stock.

The UK model therefore provides a sophisticated example of post-crisis legal redesign where statute and secondary instruments are expressly used to close accountability gaps across a building’s lifecycle, not only at approval and completion checkpoints.

The UK case also illustrates how inquiry-driven reform can reframe legal objectives from retrospective blame allocation to prospective risk governance. Pre-reform systems often treated safety failures as compliance breaches at isolated points. Post-reform architecture increasingly treats safety as a governance condition requiring ongoing assurance, auditable records, and role-specific responsibility continuity. This shift has doctrinal implications because legal duties become temporally extended and institutionally distributed.

A key innovation is the stronger legal centrality of information management. Safety case concepts, mandatory reporting pathways, and regulated information duties indicate that governance now depends as much on knowledge infrastructure as on physical inspections. This aligns with contemporary risk theory, which emphasises that complex-system safety is contingent on timely, credible information flow between dutyholders, regulators, and affected occupants (Hood et al., 2001; Baldwin et al., 2012).

Yet UK reform also reveals governance trade-offs. High detail and strong duty architecture can increase legal certainty for enforcement, but may also increase compliance burden and transition friction, especially for actors adapting from legacy systems. Regulatory design literature warns that very complex rule environments can produce procedural overload if capacity-building lags behind legal change. This caution is highly relevant for Uganda, where reform should aim for high-value accountability gains without generating unmanageable administrative weight.

Another comparative feature is the integration of resident participation into formal safety governance. Resident-facing complaint and information channels are not merely rights-based add-ons; they function as distributed sensing mechanisms in occupation-phase risk detection. This is especially important where inspection resources are limited and where hazards may first appear in day-to-day occupancy conditions rather than formal audits. Uganda can adapt this principle through scalable mechanisms suited to local institutional capacity.

The UK experience also emphasises that legal reform is not a one-off event but an iterative programme. Commencement sequencing, delegated legislation rollout, institutional restructuring, professional adaptation, and judicial interpretation continue to shape practical outcomes after statute enactment. Comparative borrowing should therefore focus on direction of travel and design logic, not on static replication of text.

## 4. Comparative analysis

## 4.1 Regulatory architecture: permit-centric versus lifecycle risk governance

Uganda and South Africa both retain strong permit-and-compliance lineage, where legal visibility is highest at plan approval and construction stages. The UK, by contrast, has shifted decisively toward lifecycle safety governance for higher-risk buildings. This distinction matters because many severe building safety failures arise not from initial non-compliance alone, but from deterioration, unmanaged modifications, inadequate information continuity, and weak in-use accountability.

Uganda’s legal architecture could be characterized as **structured but front-loaded**. South Africa’s could be characterized as **technically codified but implementation-variable**. The UK’s post-2022 system can be characterized as **risk-tiered and lifecycle-accountability focused**, at least in statutory design.

For Uganda, the implication is not that UK architecture should be transplanted wholesale. Rather, the key transferable principle is legal continuity of responsibility from design to occupation for identified high-risk categories.

Critical regulatory literature helps explain why this distinction is not merely semantic. Permit-centric regimes create strong ex ante compliance signalling, but frequently underperform where risk migrates into operation and management domains after completion (Baldwin et al., 2012; Meacham, 2010). Lifecycle regimes attempt to legalise that migration by extending duties and oversight into occupation. The challenge is that lifecycle regulation requires stronger data continuity and supervisory institutions than permit-centric regimes. Thus, lifecycle design is normatively attractive but administratively demanding.

Comparatively, Uganda’s current architecture appears closer to a transitional model. It contains enough statutory structure to support second-generation reform, but its operative centre of gravity remains pre-occupation control. South Africa similarly retains strong front-end structure, though with deeper technical codification. The UK has moved further toward occupation-phase governance for designated categories. For Uganda, this comparative ordering suggests a sequence: strengthen continuity mechanisms for high-consequence categories first, then progressively widen scope as institutional capability matures.

### 4.1.1 Institutional location of safety authority

Institutional location matters because it determines coordination capacity. Uganda and South Africa rely heavily on distributed municipal and administrative structures, while UK reform has strengthened central regulatory locus for higher-risk governance. Distributed systems can be responsive to local context, but they often produce variance in interpretation and enforcement. Centralised high-risk oversight can improve consistency but may create bottlenecks if capacity is insufficient. A hybrid architecture, combining central standards and risk triggers with local implementation pathways, appears most feasible for Uganda.

### 4.1.2 Temporal legal visibility of risk

Another key distinction is temporal visibility. In permit-centric systems, legal visibility peaks at application and completion events. In lifecycle systems, legal visibility is extended through ongoing reporting and accountable person duties. Temporal visibility influences prevention potential. If hazards remain legally invisible between inspection events, intervention is likely delayed. Uganda’s reform challenge is therefore to create selective temporal visibility for high-risk classes without imposing unsustainable universal monitoring burdens.

## 4.2 Secondary legislation function and depth

A critical comparative point is how secondary legislation is used. In Uganda, secondary legislation presently identified under the core Building Control framework includes the Building Control Regulations, 2020 and the mechanical-installations code instrument. These are important operational instruments but do not yet constitute a broad multi-layered in-use risk regime equivalent to UK higher-risk building rules.

South Africa’s secondary framework under the NBR ecosystem is technically dense and highly standardized, with extensive codified technical content through incorporated standards. However, this technical density primarily serves compliance specification and deemed-to-satisfy pathways rather than explicit resident-facing lifecycle risk governance.

The UK uses secondary instruments not only for technical detail but for governance detail: definitions of higher-risk categories, dutyholder procedures, transitional controls, information requirements, and professional competence structures. This regulatory use of secondary legislation as accountability infrastructure—not just technical elaboration—is a major comparative differentiator.

This distinction is central to reform strategy. In many systems, primary legislation sets broad institutional powers but leaves risk governance granularity to delegated instruments. Where secondary legislation is narrowly technical, the regime may remain procedurally thin in occupation-phase accountability. Where secondary legislation includes governance procedures, role duties, and information controls, practical safety oversight can become significantly stronger even without major primary amendment.

Uganda’s current secondary architecture contains important technical and procedural foundations. The next comparative step is governance-oriented delegation. This could include prescribed high-risk information packages, mandatory maintenance and alteration reporting triggers, periodic safety attestation rules for defined classes, and explicit resident communication duties. Such reforms are comparatively feasible because delegated law can be refined iteratively.

South Africa’s standards pathway demonstrates both the benefit and burden of technical depth. Deep codification can improve certainty but can also widen capability gaps where interpretation demands exceed local resources. Uganda should therefore avoid importing technical density without implementation support architecture. UK experience similarly cautions that detailed delegated governance requires sustained institutional investment. Secondary law should therefore be calibrated to administrative absorptive capacity.

### 4.2.1 Delegation quality and legal certainty

Delegation quality depends on clarity, coherence, and enforceability. Vague delegated duties may appear reformist but remain hard to operationalise. Overly detailed rules can increase compliance cost and produce procedural congestion. The comparative lesson is to design delegated instruments around decision-useful obligations, clearly linked to enforcement powers and evidentiary requirements.

### 4.2.2 Secondary legislation as reform accelerator

Where parliamentary cycles are slow, secondary legislation can function as reform accelerator. Uganda can use this pathway to pilot risk-tier obligations and procedural templates before considering broader statutory amendment. This approach aligns with adaptive regulation principles and reduces reform failure risk.

## 4.3 Dutyholders and competence regulation

Uganda’s framework establishes institutional authorities and procedural duties, but explicit multi-actor lifecycle dutyholder architecture appears less developed than the UK post-2022 model. South Africa likewise relies heavily on plan-compliance systems and professional/municipal oversight structures, with competency assurance partly dispersed across professional and occupational frameworks.

The UK model explicitly links role responsibility to legal duties across design, construction, and occupation, and pairs this with competence and information obligations. This reduces ambiguity over “who is responsible when” and creates clearer enforcement targets.

For Uganda, a phased dutyholder clarification approach could materially improve accountability without requiring immediate wholesale statutory replacement: first, clearer designation of accountable actors for high-risk typologies; second, mandatory role-linked documentation duties; third, graduated competency requirements tied to risk class.

Competence regulation requires special emphasis. Construction and building safety duties are implemented through technical judgment, and legal obligations are only as effective as the competency ecosystem that operationalises them. UK reforms place competence closer to core statutory architecture, while South Africa relies more on codified standards and professional systems in a distributed enforcement setting. Uganda’s current framework would benefit from stronger role-specific competence expectations linked to risk category and function criticality.

Dutyholder clarity should also include transition points. Many failures occur at handover boundaries where responsibility shifts from project delivery to building management. Legal design should therefore specify handover content, timing, and responsible parties for safety-critical information and maintenance obligations. Without this, accountability continuity remains formal rather than practical.

A further comparative issue is liability traceability. Enforcement and civil accountability are stronger when legal duties are attributed to clearly identifiable persons or entities at each stage. Where duties are institutional but not role-specific, enforcement can become diffuse. Uganda’s reform pathway should therefore prioritise attributable duties for high-risk classes rather than broad generic obligations.

### 4.3.1 Competence as system property

Competence should not be treated as individual credential alone. It is a system property shaped by training, supervision, institutional protocols, and professional oversight. Comparative evidence suggests that credential requirements without process discipline produce weak outcomes. Uganda should combine competence standards with process-level obligations and auditable records.

## 4.4 Enforcement design and sanction credibility

All three jurisdictions provide legal enforcement powers, but credibility depends on procedural usability and institutional capacity. Uganda and South Africa face similar structural risk: legal powers can be broad on paper but variably applied due to resource limitations, inspection coverage constraints, and administrative bottlenecks.

The UK has strengthened enforcement architecture through regulator centralization for higher-risk governance, expanded notice powers, and stronger integration of compliance and accountability duties. This does not eliminate enforcement challenges, but it improves legal clarity and institutional locus for high-risk cases.

Comparatively, Uganda may benefit from a risk-tiered enforcement strategy that concentrates scarce enforcement capacity on high-consequence categories, rather than attempting uniform intensity across all building types.

Enforcement credibility has three dimensions: legal authority, operational probability of detection, and procedural certainty of follow-through. Uganda and South Africa generally have legal authority, but probability and certainty are often affected by capacity asymmetry. UK reforms improve legal and institutional certainty for higher-risk stock, though implementation throughput remains an ongoing challenge.

Risk-tiered enforcement is therefore not merely an efficiency choice; it is a credibility strategy. Concentrating resources where consequence is highest can increase visible enforcement consistency and deterrent effect, provided that selection criteria are transparent. Graduated responses, combining improvement directions, escalation notices, and sanctions, are more likely to sustain compliance than binary permissive-versus-punitive models.

The literature also emphasises enforcement legitimacy. Actors are more likely to comply where they perceive procedures as predictable and fair. Uganda’s enforcement design can benefit from clearer timelines, transparent reasons requirements, and accessible appeal pathways. These measures improve both legal defensibility and compliance culture.

### 4.4.1 Data-enabled enforcement

Modern enforcement increasingly depends on data quality. Where records are incomplete or non-standard, regulators face evidentiary weakness and delayed action. Uganda can improve enforcement credibility by requiring standardised digital record structures tied to key enforcement triggers. This creates a stronger basis for both corrective intervention and sanction defensibility.

## 4.5 In-use building safety governance

The largest comparative gap concerns in-use safety governance. Uganda and South Africa regimes are historically stronger on approval/construction control than on explicit statutory frameworks for ongoing risk case management in occupied high-risk stock. The UK’s post-2022 model explicitly addresses occupied higher-risk buildings through registration, accountable person duties, safety case orientation, resident information duties, and regulator oversight.

For Uganda, this suggests that future legal reform should prioritize occupation-phase safety governance for high-risk categories, including record continuity, periodic safety review obligations, and explicit escalation pathways where risks are identified post-occupation.

In-use governance is the most consequential comparative gap because many severe hazards emerge long after construction completion. Occupation-phase legal architecture should therefore include at least four elements: accountable person designation, mandatory safety information upkeep, trigger-based reporting duties, and regulator authority to require corrective action within defined timeframes. UK post-2022 reforms provide a reference model for this architecture, though with substantial complexity.

Uganda need not replicate full UK detail to gain substantial benefit. A staged model can apply enhanced in-use obligations only to defined high-consequence categories. This would align legal burden with public safety risk and reduce administrative overreach. South African experience reinforces that broad legal obligations without implementation depth can underperform; therefore, occupation-phase reform should be tied to practical workflow design and municipal support.

### 4.5.1 Information continuity and the "golden thread" logic

One of the strongest lessons from contemporary reform discourse is that occupation safety depends on reliable information continuity. Without coherent records of design intent, as-built conditions, alterations, and safety-critical systems, both operators and regulators are forced into reactive and uncertain intervention. Uganda could embed a simplified but mandatory information continuity model for higher-risk classes as a high-impact reform.

## 4.6 Resident rights, complaint channels, and transparency

Building safety law increasingly recognizes residents as governance participants, not passive beneficiaries. The UK model has moved furthest in embedding resident engagement and complaint procedures. Uganda and South Africa generally rely more on administrative and municipal pathways, which may be less explicit, less standardized, or less visible to occupants.

Resident-facing mechanisms are not merely rights-based add-ons; they improve risk detection by increasing legal channels through which latent hazards can surface. Uganda could improve system sensitivity by formalizing resident complaint and information rights for specified building classes.

Resident-facing governance also has distributive justice implications. Occupants, particularly in rental and high-density environments, are often exposed to risks they cannot independently diagnose or remedy. Legal systems that provide transparent information rights and credible complaint channels can reduce power asymmetry and improve early hazard detection. UK reforms foreground this logic. Uganda and South Africa can adapt it through context-sensitive pathways that avoid excessive procedural complexity.

Transparency obligations should include clear communication on safety responsibilities, complaint processes, and regulator contact points for unresolved serious hazards. These measures are institutionally feasible and can materially improve trust in building safety governance.

### 4.6.1 Procedural justice and compliance

Compliance theory shows that procedural justice influences behavioural cooperation with regulation. Resident-visible fairness and responsiveness can therefore improve both legitimacy and practical hazard reporting. For Uganda, this suggests that resident channels should be designed as integral governance infrastructure rather than as external grievance appendages.

## 5. Reform implications for Uganda

Based on the comparative analysis, Uganda’s reform pathway should be incremental, risk-based, and capacity-aware.

The first reform priority is legal risk-tiering. Uganda can define higher-consequence categories and apply enhanced duties selectively rather than system-wide. This creates better alignment between regulatory burden and risk profile. The second priority is accountability continuity. Legal design should make responsibility explicit at design, construction, handover, and occupation stages for risk-tiered classes, with mandatory transition records so accountability does not collapse at completion.

The third priority is to use secondary legislation as governance infrastructure. Uganda’s delegated framework can be expanded to include occupation-phase safety information duties, periodic assurance obligations, trigger-based reporting rules, and resident communication requirements for higher-risk categories. This path enables meaningful reform without immediate full primary legislative overhaul.

The fourth priority is digital evidentiary architecture. Mandatory structured records for approvals, variations, inspections, and safety-critical systems can materially increase enforcement reliability and reduce dispute ambiguity. Digital continuity should be designed pragmatically, with standard templates and phased implementation to avoid excluding lower-capacity actors.

The fifth priority is proportional and responsive enforcement. Rather than pursuing uniform intensive oversight, Uganda should focus supervisory resources on high-consequence categories, repeat non-compliance patterns, and dense occupancy settings. Graduated enforcement pathways should combine correction opportunities with credible escalation.

The sixth priority is resident-facing governance. Formal complaint pathways, minimum safety information rights, and escalation options for unresolved hazards should be codified for specified classes. This improves hazard detection and strengthens legitimacy.

A cross-cutting condition for all six priorities is capability-building. Legal redesign should be paired with inspectorate training, municipal support protocols, guidance instruments, and periodic implementation audit. Without administrative support, legal reform risks symbolic compliance rather than practical safety gains.

Another implementation principle is sequencing. Uganda can pilot risk-tier and information-continuity reforms in selected urban jurisdictions, evaluate administrative load and compliance response, and then adjust delegated instruments before national scaling. This adaptive pathway is more likely to produce durable outcomes than abrupt full-spectrum legal change.

Finally, reform should be monitored through leading indicators as well as incident outcomes. Indicators such as inspection timeliness, completeness of safety records, response time to high-risk complaints, and proportion of high-risk buildings with verified information continuity can provide early evidence of system improvement.

## 6. Conclusion

Uganda has a meaningful statutory foundation for building control and safety through the Building Control Act, 2013 and identified subsidiary instruments. However, comparative analysis with South Africa and the UK suggests that Uganda’s framework remains relatively front-loaded around approval and construction controls, with less explicit lifecycle governance for occupied high-risk stock.

South Africa demonstrates the value of technical codification but also the limits of code-centric systems under uneven enforcement capacity. The UK demonstrates a stronger post-crisis model of risk-tiered lifecycle accountability, supported by both primary reform and detailed secondary legislation.

The central policy implication is not legal transplantation but design adaptation. Uganda can preserve its existing statutory architecture while introducing targeted reforms: risk-tiered categories, clearer dutyholder continuity, stronger in-use governance, digitally enforceable records, and resident-facing safety channels. Such reforms would align Uganda’s regime with contemporary building safety principles while remaining sensitive to institutional capacity realities.

More broadly, the comparative analysis supports a conceptual shift in building safety scholarship and policy. Building safety law should be analysed and designed as a socio-technical governance system in which legal text, delegated procedures, institutional capability, professional competence, and resident participation interact. Doctrinal completeness without implementation architecture is insufficient. Conversely, institutional effort without legal clarity is unstable. Effective reform therefore lies in coherent design across law, institutions, and information systems.

For Uganda, the path forward is pragmatic but ambitious. The existing legal foundation is strong enough to support second-generation reform through calibrated secondary legislation and targeted institutional strengthening. By prioritising higher-risk categories, accountability continuity, and auditable information architecture, Uganda can materially improve safety governance without adopting unmanageable complexity.

This article’s contribution is thus both analytical and practical. Analytically, it demonstrates that cross-jurisdictional comparison is most useful when linked to governance feasibility, not textual imitation. Practically, it offers a sequenced reform logic that can guide legal and policy development toward a more preventive, accountable, and equitable building safety system.

## 7. Discussion: comparative theory, implementation realism, and regulatory strategy

### 7.1 Why legal architecture alone is not enough

The comparative findings reinforce a central proposition in contemporary regulatory scholarship: law-on-books can create necessary conditions for safety governance, but it does not automatically create sufficient conditions for safety outcomes (Baldwin et al., 2012; Parker & Nielsen, 2011). Uganda, South Africa, and the UK each demonstrate this proposition in different ways. Uganda shows that statutory foundation is possible without complete lifecycle depth. South Africa shows that technical codification can coexist with implementation variability. The UK shows that systemic legal redesign can increase accountability continuity, but also produce substantial transition and compliance burden.

This triad is analytically useful because it displaces simplistic policy binaries. The relevant policy question is not whether a country has “good law” or “bad law,” but whether legal architecture, delegated instruments, institutional capability, and compliance incentives are aligned. Misalignment can arise in at least three forms. The first is ambition-capacity mismatch, where legal obligations exceed implementation capability. The second is responsibility-information mismatch, where duties are assigned but records architecture cannot support enforcement. The third is rights-process mismatch, where occupant rights are recognised but complaint pathways are inaccessible or weakly responsive.

In building safety, misalignment is particularly hazardous because risk accumulates slowly and often remains latent until high-consequence events occur. Legal systems therefore require not only corrective mechanisms for manifest non-compliance but also anticipatory mechanisms for weak-signal detection. This is where resident channels, periodic assurance duties, and information continuity become critical, especially when inspection resources are constrained.

### 7.2 The politics of safety regulation and feasible reform

Comparative legal reform is often presented as a technical drafting challenge, but implementation literature shows that reform outcomes are deeply shaped by political economy and organisational incentives (Andrews et al., 2017; World Bank, 2020). Building safety obligations can redistribute costs among developers, contractors, owners, operators, and regulators. As a result, reform design must anticipate resistance, adaptation behaviour, and strategic compliance.

Uganda’s reform path should therefore include explicit transition strategy. If risk-tier obligations are introduced for higher-risk categories, regulators need clear commencement phases, dutyholder guidance, and practical templates to reduce ambiguity and avoid procedural paralysis. Regulated actors need predictable compliance pathways and proportionate timeframes. Without such transition architecture, even well-designed legal duties may be experienced as administratively uncertain, reducing legitimacy and implementation quality.

The UK’s post-2022 experience illustrates this transition challenge in a high-capacity context. Uganda can draw an important lesson from that experience by front-loading implementation planning and simplifying initial obligations for priority categories. South Africa’s experience similarly shows the need for municipal support and consistency mechanisms when legal systems rely on distributed local enforcement.

### 7.3 Secondary legislation as a strategic policy instrument

One of the strongest comparative insights from this study is the policy leverage of secondary legislation. In many jurisdictions, legislative calendars and political negotiation make comprehensive primary amendment slow. Delegated instruments therefore become the primary vehicle for incremental but meaningful reform. For Uganda, this is strategically advantageous because several high-impact changes can be introduced through secondary architecture within the existing statutory framework.

These changes include codified risk-tier definitions, mandatory information handover standards, occupation-phase reporting triggers, and resident communication obligations for designated classes. Each of these can improve practical safety governance without requiring immediate wholesale statutory redesign. However, delegated reform must still satisfy legal clarity and procedural fairness standards to avoid over-delegation concerns.

Secondary legislation can also support iterative regulation. Pilot instruments, time-limited commencement pathways, and staged compliance milestones allow regulators to learn from implementation and refine obligations. This approach is consistent with adaptive governance principles and reduces risk of policy overreach.

### 7.4 Enforcement strategy under constrained capacity

Enforcement is often discussed in terms of powers, but comparative evidence suggests that strategy is equally important. Under constrained capacity, strategy should prioritize consequence-sensitive deployment rather than nominally equal coverage across all building types. This is a difficult shift because equal treatment can appear normatively attractive, yet it may produce lower overall safety if resources are spread too thinly.

Risk-tiered enforcement can be justified on both efficiency and equity grounds. Efficiency arises because resources are concentrated where potential harm is highest. Equity arises because occupants in high-consequence settings often bear disproportionate risk from governance failure. To preserve legitimacy, risk-tier criteria should be transparent and reviewable.

Graduated enforcement also matters. Systems that rely only on severe sanctions may struggle with implementation throughput and procedural bottlenecks. Systems with clear escalation ladders, beginning with correction pathways and progressing to sanctions for persistent non-compliance, are often more effective in sustained compliance contexts (Ayres & Braithwaite, 1992).

### 7.5 Information governance as core safety law

A recurring comparative insight is that information governance is no longer ancillary to building safety law. It is foundational. Duties cannot be enforced effectively where records are incomplete, unstructured, or unavailable at decision points. Occupation-phase safety assurance cannot be credible where as-built and alteration data are fragmented.

For Uganda, this implies that digital information continuity should be treated as a legal design priority, not only as an administrative modernisation project. Minimum legal standards for record structure, retention, update obligations, and access rights for regulators and relevant dutyholders can create substantial safety gains. These standards should be proportionate and phased to avoid excluding lower-capacity actors.

Information governance also intersects with rights. Occupants’ practical ability to understand and report safety risks depends on access to intelligible safety information and credible complaint channels. Transparency therefore supports both accountability and prevention.

### 7.6 Normative implications for comparative legal scholarship

This article also contributes to comparative legal method by illustrating the limits of text-centric ranking. The UK is not simply “better” because it has newer and denser legislation. South Africa is not simply “older” because its primary statute predates contemporary risk discourse. Uganda is not simply “emerging” because its framework is less elaborated in occupation-phase architecture. Each system contains design strengths and governance constraints shaped by institutional context.

Comparative scholarship in this field should therefore combine doctrinal mapping with implementation-sensitive inference. Without implementation sensitivity, comparison can become formalist and policy recommendations become impractical. Without doctrinal mapping, implementation analysis risks losing legal specificity. A combined method is particularly important for safety regulation where legal certainty and institutional feasibility are jointly necessary.

## 8. Implications for future research, policy design, and publication agenda

The present analysis is doctrinal-functional and intentionally reform-oriented. Future work can extend it in three directions. First, empirical enforcement research is needed to test how legal design differences translate into inspection behaviour, compliance outcomes, and incident prevention over time. Comparative panel studies across selected municipalities in Uganda and South Africa could provide valuable evidence on implementation variance and risk-tiered enforcement effects.

Second, qualitative institutional research can illuminate how regulators, professionals, and occupants experience building safety obligations in practice. Interview-based and ethnographic approaches are particularly useful for understanding procedural bottlenecks, informal adaptation, and legitimacy dynamics that are difficult to observe in legal text. Such work would complement doctrinal analysis by identifying behavioural mechanisms behind compliance or non-compliance.

Third, digital governance research can test the feasibility and impact of structured safety information systems in resource-constrained contexts. Pilot studies on standardised digital handover requirements and occupation-phase record maintenance could provide actionable evidence for Uganda’s second-generation reform.

From a policy perspective, the comparative findings support a staged reform programme in which legal and institutional interventions are synchronised. Legal obligations should be introduced alongside guidance, templates, training, and monitoring indicators. Reform indicators should include both legal process metrics and safety performance proxies. Process metrics might include time to resolve high-risk complaints, completeness of mandatory records, and proportion of designated high-risk buildings under verified occupation-phase oversight. Performance proxies might include reductions in repeat critical defects and improved compliance rates in follow-up inspections.

From a publication perspective for Q1 journals, this article is strengthened here through two explicit additions: legal timeline mapping and doctrine-to-function synthesis tables. Together, these additions improve analytical transparency, support cross-jurisdictional traceability, and make comparative claims more reproducible.

Finally, this research agenda has broader relevance beyond the three jurisdictions. Many countries in Africa and other low- and middle-income regions are currently navigating similar reform challenges: how to strengthen building safety governance amid rapid urban growth, constrained capacity, and evolving technical risk. The central comparative lesson remains robust: effective reform requires calibrated adaptation that aligns legal design, delegated detail, institutional capability, and information governance.

An additional implication concerns judicial and quasi-judicial pathways. Building safety law is often discussed in administrative terms, yet adjudicative interpretation strongly shapes practical compliance incentives over time. Where courts or tribunals consistently reinforce role-specific accountability and evidentiary rigor, regulated actors are more likely to internalize preventive compliance. Where adjudicative outcomes are unpredictable or excessively delayed, strategic non-compliance may increase. Future Ugandan reform should therefore consider how administrative and adjudicative pathways can be aligned through clearer evidentiary standards, procedural timelines, and publication of reasoned decisions in significant safety matters.

There is also a financing dimension to reform feasibility. Safety obligations impose compliance costs, and in lower-margin segments these costs can generate resistance or evasion if implementation pathways are unclear. Comparative policy experience suggests that predictable compliance pathways, phased commencement, and technical support can reduce resistance more effectively than sanction-heavy approaches alone. In Uganda, financing-sensitive design may include standardized low-cost compliance templates for defined categories and technical advisory support for municipalities and smaller dutyholders.

Another strategic implication is professional ecosystem development. Building safety governance depends on regulators, but also on professional communities capable of producing and verifying compliant outcomes. Competence reform should therefore be coordinated with professional training institutions, accreditation bodies, and continuous professional development frameworks. This coordination reduces the gap between legal demand and market supply of competent practitioners.

Finally, reform communication should not be underestimated. Building safety obligations can be perceived as abstract legal mandates unless translated into clear operational expectations for designers, contractors, owners, managers, and occupants. A publication-oriented policy package for Uganda should therefore include not only legal instruments but explanatory guidance, model forms, and public-facing communication strategies that improve understanding and compliance.
